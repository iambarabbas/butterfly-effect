Finishing What Our Mothers Started

Someone offered me an idea the other day that I have not been able to stop thinking about: maybe we are here to finish what our mothers started. The more I sit with that idea, the more I find myself looking backward with gratitude.

I think about my mother, my grandmothers, my great-grandmothers, my mother-in-law, and all the women who came before us. Women who lived through war, the Depression, financial dependence, limited rights, rigid religious expectations, and cultural rules that told them where they belonged and how much of themselves they were allowed to become.

And still, they moved forward. When I look at the women in my own family, I do not see women who failed to do enough. I see women who did extraordinary things within the circumstances they were given. I see resourcefulness, intelligence, courage, creativity, faithfulness, loyalty, and determination. I see women who kept opening the door a little wider for the women who would come after them.

My great-grandmother on my dad’s side had to figure out how to feed her children during the Depression. She taught herself how to type and earned money typing menus for a local restaurant. She also assisted the local doctor, helping bring babies into the world. I think about what that must have required of her. There was no easy path, and probably very little recognition. She simply saw what needed to be done and found a way to do it. She used her intelligence, her hands, and her courage to help her family survive while also serving other families in her community.

On my dad’s side, my grandmother wanted to go to college at a time when higher education was not an obvious or accessible path for many women. She arrived almost before she had even been accepted, only to discover that she did not have enough money to pay for it. Instead of giving up, she called her sister, and together they made a plan. One sister would work for a year while the other attended school. Then they would switch. They took turns sacrificing for one another until they had both earned their degrees. It took them six years, but they made it. I’ve always loved that story because it wasn’t simply about determination. It was about two women believing in each other’s future enough to carry the burden together. They understood that sometimes moving forward isn’t something we do alone. Sometimes one woman works so another woman can learn, and then they trade places until both have crossed the finish line.

My maternal grandmother was the secretary and organist at the church she and my grandfather faithfully served, but those titles hardly capture everything she did. She organized much of the life of the church, but she was also the history keeper of our family. She preserved birthdays, anniversaries, photographs, stories, milestones, and the little details that made people feel remembered. She never forgot an important date. She understood that people feel loved when they are remembered.

Then there is my mother. When I was growing up, I always remember her taking classes. Learning wasn’t something she did once. It was simply part of who she was. She took dance classes. She sang in the opera and always sang at church. She was an avid reader and was intentional about bringing music, literature, and art into our lives. She wanted us to encounter beauty. She wanted us to understand that the world was much larger than the one immediately in front of us. She also planned much of our international travel all on a very tight budget, giving my brothers and me the opportunity to experience different countries, cultures, and ways of living. Because of her, we learned to see the world through more than one set of eyes.

Then, when my brothers and I reached college age and our family couldn’t afford to send all of us, she went back to school and earned her master’s degree so she could increase her earning power and help pay for our education.

My mother-in-law created another kind of inheritance. She built friendships with women that lasted a lifetime. She understood the importance of women staying connected to one another, not only through the responsibilities of marriage and family.

She and her friends figured out a way to travel to Hawaii after college, and later in life they traveled all over the US and Europe together. They kept choosing friendship. They kept making room for joy. They kept reminding one another that a woman’s life could continue expanding through every season.

When I look back, I can see that each woman handed something forward: survival, education, memory, organization, faithfulness, independence, music, literature, art, curiosity, a wider view of the world, friendship, loyalty, adventure, and the knowledge that women need one another. I am the recipient of all of it, and that changes the way I think about the past.

It is easy to look backward with the knowledge and freedom of our own generation and wonder why the women before us did not do more. Why they did not speak up. Why they stayed in certain situations. Why they accepted religious teachings, marital roles, or cultural expectations that now seem so limiting.

But they were not living with the choices, language, resources, and awareness that I have now. They were living inside systems that were designed to hold women back.

For generations, women could not always sign for a loan on a car or a house without a husband or male relative. They had limited access to credit, property, education, and financial independence. Many women were expected to marry, and once married, they were expected to stay, no matter how lonely, diminished, or trapped they felt. They were taught to be agreeable, demure, quiet, supportive, and grateful. They were often told that a good woman did not ask for too much, speak too loudly, earn too much, lead too visibly, or challenge her husband’s authority. Christianity, particularly in its more rigid and patriarchal forms, often reinforced those expectations. Women were told that submission was holy, obedience was love, suffering was faithfulness, and male leadership was God’s design.

Many women were not simply making personal choices. They were navigating entire religious, legal, economic, and cultural structures that limited the choices available to them.

That does not mean every choice was right, and it does not mean we excuse addiction, abuse, cruelty, or harm. Some cycles must be named honestly, and some must end completely.

But in families where women were trying to love, grow, protect, provide, and create something better, perhaps we can learn to see them with gratitude and compassion.

They were moving forward while carrying the weight of systems that told them not to.

My great-grandmother moved the story forward from where her mother had left it. My grandmother moved it forward from where her mother had left it. My mother carried it forward again, and my mother-in-law expanded the story through enduring friendship and a willingness to keep experiencing the world. Because of them, I get to begin farther down the road, and that fills me with hope.

I do not need to question the women in my life for failing to teach me things they had never been taught themselves. I do not need to judge my matriarchs for the freedoms they did not yet know how to claim.

I can honor how far they came and be grateful for what they gave me. Then I can ask the more important question: What is my responsibility now?

I live in a generation shaped by the women’s rights movement, the Me Too movement, and an ongoing cultural reckoning around equality, power, marriage, sexuality, religion, money, and work.

I have choices, language, and access the women before me did not have. That means I also have responsibility. My responsibility is to use my voice where women were once expected to remain quiet. It is to stand up for my rights within my marriage and not confuse love with disappearing. It is to understand our finances, participate fully in decisions, and take responsibility for the future of my family. It is to know what we own, what we owe, where our money is invested, and how decisions are being made.

It is to claim an equal seat at work, at home, and in the places where women have long done the labor without being given the authority. It is to question the religious teachings that diminished women.

For me, that has meant finding my way out of evangelical Christianity and the purity movement. It has meant recognizing the ways those teachings encouraged women to distrust their bodies, silence their instincts, surrender their authority, and confuse obedience with goodness.

It has meant recognizing that teachings presented as eternal truth were often deeply shaped by culture, patriarchy, and fear. It has meant learning that faith does not require me to become smaller and trusting my own spiritual wisdom.

Women have always been the spiritual backbone of families and communities, even when institutions refused to recognize them as leaders. They have carried prayer, ritual, compassion, memory, intuition, and moral courage from one generation to the next.

Perhaps women were never meant to stand behind the spiritual life of a family. Perhaps we were meant to help lead it all along.

My responsibility is also to keep creating meaningful friendships with other women, to make room for women outside the roles of wife, mother, daughter, or caretaker, and to travel together, laugh together, tell the truth together, and witness one another’s lives. Friendship is not an extra. It is part of the structure that helps women remain whole.

My responsibility is not to reject the women who came before me. It is to honor them by continuing their work. Their lives are not stories of failure. They are stories of movement. Every woman pushed against something, created a little more space, and handed the next generation a few more choices than she had been given.

Because my great-grandmother found a way to feed her children, I inherited resilience. Because my grandmother worked her way through college, I inherited a belief in education and independence. Because my maternal grandmother preserved our family’s story, I inherited memory and belonging. Because my mother filled our lives with music, books, art, travel, and beauty, I inherited curiosity and a wider view of the world. Because my mother-in-law invested in lifelong friendship and adventure, I inherited another picture of what a woman’s life could be.

I did not arrive here on my own. I am standing on the lives, prayers, sacrifices, endurance, imagination, friendships, and courage of generations of women. They brought me this far. Now it is my responsibility to keep going.

I want to become more free, not only for myself, but for the women who will come after me. I want to make choices my grandmothers could not make, speak truths my mother may not have had the words to say, and use the access, authority, and opportunities of my generation with courage. I want to pass forward not only survival, but wholeness; not only education, but self-trust; not only beauty, but freedom; not only faith, but spiritual authority; not only family, but friendship; and not only endurance, but the freedom to choose.

Perhaps this is how we honor our grandmothers and mothers best — not by expecting them to have completed the whole journey or resenting them for where they had to stop, but by understanding what they were up against, seeing how bravely they moved within the limits of their time, and receiving what they gave us with gratitude while carrying it forward with responsibility. They began the work. They moved the story. They opened the door. Now it is my turn to walk through it and hold it open for the women coming behind me.
