When Helping Becomes Rescuing

During one of our days in the Amazon, our group went on a long hike through the rainforest.

It was raining, the trail was steep and slippery, and some of the women were struggling. Eventually, one woman fainted.

I became furious.

I worried that someone would fall, break a hip, have a stroke, or need medical help we could not possibly reach. I was angry at the leaders for putting people in danger, and angry that no one else seemed as concerned as I was.

When we returned, we gathered to talk about the experience.

I knew I was too upset to speak kindly, so I passed.

Then I listened as the other women described the hike as transformative. They talked about discovering that they could do hard things. They talked about accepting help, being supported, and realizing they were stronger than they had believed.

I remember thinking, What?

We had all been on the same hike, but we had experienced something completely different.

Then a difficult question came to me.

How often do I rush in to save someone from the very experience that might help them grow?

I have always been a helper. I notice what people need. I step in. I organize, encourage, solve, carry, and protect.

Those are real gifts.

But even a gift has a shadow.

Helping becomes rescuing when I assume I know what another person needs. It becomes control when I cannot tolerate watching someone struggle. It becomes less about their growth and more about relieving my own discomfort.

That does not mean we ignore suffering or withhold help. It means we pause long enough to ask whether our help is strengthening someone or quietly taking away their opportunity to discover their own strength.

Sometimes love steps forward.

Sometimes love stands nearby.

Sometimes love trusts another person enough to let them take the next step themselves.

Helping is not about becoming the hero of someone else's story. It is about offering support without taking away their dignity, agency, or opportunity to grow.

Where might you be rescuing someone who needs your support, but also needs the chance to discover what they can do?
